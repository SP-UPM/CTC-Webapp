1.1 Purpose, audience and outcomes

The word critically is everywhere in Malaysian higher education. Critical Thinking, as the competency is defined, is rare. The difference is not effort:

  1. Analysis takes the question as given and answers it well.
  2. Critical Thinking interrogates the question. It requires a student to ask how the evidence in front of them was produced, what the framing takes for granted, where their own conclusion is weakest, and how strong a judgement the evidence will actually bear.

THE QUESTION THIS MODULE ANSWERS

An examiner will not ask whether your students thought critically. They will ask: which document shows a student examining the weakness of their own conclusion, and what mark that earned?

The module has five learning outcomes, each tied to a specific piece of work you will have produced by the end of the day. ML = learning outcome.

1.2.2 The national mandate, and why PLO12 exists

This is not only an international agenda. The Malaysian Qualifications Framework (MQF), Second Edition 2024, makes it a national requirement. Among its four new emphases — Values-Based Education, Flexible Learning Pathways, the Global Sustainability Agenda (GSA), and harmonisation with sectoral frameworks — the GSA directs higher education providers to embed the Sustainable Development Goals and sustainability competencies into the curriculum. MQF 2024 integrates these competencies into its five clusters of learning outcomes rather than treating sustainability as a separate subject, which is exactly the logic this guidebook follows: existing content, taught and assessed so that it also develops a sustainability competency.

UPM's PLO12 is the institutional route through which that mandate is made traceable at course level. All eight competencies, including Critical Thinking, are assessed under PLO12 — never scattered across other PLOs, and never left ungraded as “soft skills”. A dedicated PLO makes three things possible.

  1. Traceability. Every competency claim resolves to a PLO12-mapped CLO, a rubric criterion, and a marked script.
  2. Attainment data. PLO12 attainment is computed from the competency rubric criteria alone, not diluted by unrelated criteria.
  3. Programme-level coverage. Aggregate PLO12 data across courses shows which of the eight the programme actually develops, and which it only claims.

1.2.5 Critical Thinking's partner competency

Exactly two competencies are covered per selected course, so Critical Thinking always has a partner. Two rules govern the choice.

  1. Choose a partner the content genuinely demands. The Also develops column at 3.2 shows which competency each method naturally produces alongside Critical Thinking; that is the shortest route to a defensible pair.
  2. Integrated Problem-solving is restricted. It belongs to capstone, final-year project, industrial training and clinical practice only. Mid-programme it is assessed as emerging with the rubric capped at level 3, and that must be declared in the course outline.

DO NOT CONFUSE CRITICAL THINKING WITH ITS NEIGHBOURS

Critical versus Normative. Critical asks whether a claim, inference or framing is supportable. Normative asks whether acting on it is right, fair or desirable, and for whom. A single sustainability case can involve both; the marker must be clear which reasoning is being scored.

Critical versus scepticism. Rejecting everything requires no reasoning. Criterion 3 is what separates the two: critical thinking turns the same scrutiny on the student's own position, and cynicism never does.

2.1 The definition: three abilities

UNESCO (2017)

Critical thinking competency: the ability to question norms, practices and opinions; to reflect on one's own values, perceptions and actions; and to take a position in the sustainability discourse.
#ABILITYWHAT IT DEMANDS OF STUDENT WORK
1Question norms, practices and opinionsThe claim, the evidence behind it and the framing that made it look settled must all be interrogated — not only the conclusion.
2Reflect on one's own values, perceptions and actionsThe scrutiny must be turned inward: the student's own conclusion must be shown to have a weakest point, and something must be done about it.
3Take a position in the sustainability discourseA judgement must actually be reached, and its strength must match the quality and limits of the evidence.

THE SHIFT

From evaluating the answer to interrogating the question. A student who evaluates well but never asks how the evidence was produced, or never turns the same scrutiny on their own conclusion, has not yet demonstrated this competency.

2.2 What Critical Thinking is not

CONFUSIONWHY IT FAILSASK INSTEAD
Critical Thinking is recallThe student reproduces information accurately. Accuracy is a knowledge outcome, and it is assessed elsewhere.How was this information generated, and what can it support?
Critical Thinking is opinionA preference stated fluently is still a preference. Nothing in it can be placed on four levels except confidence.What evidence, and what assumption, is that position resting on?
Critical Thinking is scepticismRejecting everything requires no reasoning and is not scrutiny. Cynicism never turns on the cynic's own position.Apply the same scrutiny to your own conclusion. Where is it weakest?
Critical Thinking is the word “critically”A verb in the task does not specify observable reasoning. “Critically discuss” is answerable from the lecture notes.Which analytical moves must the student actually perform, and in writing?
Critical Thinking is the correct conclusionComplex sustainability problems support more than one defensible conclusion. Marking the verdict marks agreement with the lecturer.Is the conclusion proportionate to the quality and limits of the evidence?

2.3 Which evidence can carry it

UNESCO expresses learning objectives in three domains. Where a competency principally sits determines what kind of evidence can carry it, and therefore rules out methods before you have chosen one.

  • Cognitive

    reasoning about claims, evidence and inference · main role

  • Socio-emotional

    reflecting on one's own position · supporting role

  • Behavioural

    acting on a bounded judgement · minor role

Critical Thinking is mainly cognitive, with a socio-emotional element in criterion 3. That makes it the mirror image of Collaboration, and it changes where the design usually fails. Collaboration fails on attribution: a group produced the evidence and no mark can be given to a person. Critical Thinking rarely fails on attribution, because the reasoning is written by one student. It fails on demand: the task did not require the reasoning, so a well-prepared student scored full marks without doing any of it.

METHODS THAT CANNOT PROVIDE SUFFICIENT EVIDENCE

A multiple-choice test, a recall examination, a “critically discuss” essay with no specified moves, a literature summary and a group product with no individual reasoning trace cannot evidence Critical Thinking, however the question is phrased. They produce knowledge evidence, and no rubric repairs that.

The fix. The evidence must be a written record in which the student interrogates named evidence, names an assumption in the framing, identifies the weakest point in their own conclusion, and states a judgement whose strength matches what the evidence supports.

2.4 Critical Thinking across fourteen clusters

Critical Thinking cannot be practised on a problem with one right answer. It needs a claim that is confidently made, supported by real but incomplete evidence, and open to more than one defensible reading. Your course already contains one; find it with four questions.

FINDING THE CONTESTABLE CLAIM

  1. Name one topic you already teach.
  2. What claim about it is asserted confidently in industry, policy, the media or a textbook?
  3. What evidence is offered for that claim, and how was that evidence produced?
  4. Could a well-informed person reach a different defensible conclusion from the same evidence? State the two conclusions in one sentence each.

If question 4 fails, the claim is settled and the topic is a knowledge topic. Choose another week. If question 3 cannot be answered, you do not yet have evidence for students to interrogate — find the study, dataset, datasheet, report or record that the claim actually rests on.

2.5 The four rubric criteria

The three abilities condense into three observable behaviours, and each behaviour becomes one rubric criterion. A fourth criterion is added for what the student does with the analysis. These four names are used throughout the rest of this document, in the design matrix and in every rubric.

WHY OBSERVABLE BEHAVIOURS, NOT DEFINITIONS

A definition tells you what to say in week one. An observable behaviour tells you what to look for when marking. The three behaviours below are the operationalised form of the abilities in 2.1 — the same competency, written so a marker can act on it.

2Identifies an assumption inside the framingFraming and assumptions

  • 2

    Can the student identify the weakest point in their own conclusion, and revise, qualify or defend it?

  • 4

    Is the conclusion proportionate to the evidence, with the degree of confidence stated?

NUMBERING, AND WHAT TO CALL THEM

Where Critical Thinking is the first competency in a course, these are criteria 1 to 4. Where it is the second, they are numbered 5 to 8 in the alignment table, so that a reviewer can follow a competency to a criterion to a script. Always name the criterion as well as numbering it.

Criteria 1 to 3 operationalise the three observable behaviours in the UPM ESD Guidebook, which themselves render the abilities in the source definition. UNESCO states one ability, question norms, practices and opinions, where this rubric has two criteria — the split is a UPM decision, made because evidence quality and framing are separately markable and students commonly reach one without the other. Say so in your course file rather than presenting the four-criterion structure as a source requirement.

2.5.1 One claim, four criteria

The same claim, read through each criterion in turn. This is the example to use with students in week one.

THE CLAIM

Online learning is more environmentally sustainable than face-to-face learning.
CRITERIONWHAT THE STUDENT ASKS
1 · EvidenceHow was environmental impact measured? Were devices, data-centre energy and avoided transport all counted, and over what period? What does the study not measure at all?
2 · FramingIs sustainability being defined only as carbon? What becomes less visible under that framing — equipment lifecycles, e-waste, access, the emissions of a household heated all day?
3 · Own positionIf I conclude that online learning has lower emissions, what is the weakest part of my conclusion — and do I now narrow it, qualify it, or defend it?
4 · JudgementDoes the evidence justify “always”, “usually”, or only “under these specified conditions”? How confident am I entitled to be?

WATCH OUT

A student who answers criteria 1 and 2 well and stops there has produced a critique. The competency is not complete until 3 and 4 are present: the scrutiny turned inward, and a bounded judgement actually reached. Criterion 3 is what separates critical thinking from cynicism, and almost nothing else in a curriculum assesses it.

3.1 Three rules for any activity

NON-NEGOTIABLE

  1. Build the contestability in. If a well-prepared student can complete the task from the notes, or if the evidence points only one way, the activity develops nothing.
  2. Use two linked activities. Once produces a story. Twice, spaced, with focused feedback between, produces an ability. Give students a practice activity, then feedback, then a later assessed activity.
  3. Turn the scrutiny inward. Something each student personally wrote about the weakest point of their own conclusion must survive the activity. Decide what it is before you run it — this is the rule that produces criterion 3, and it is the one most often omitted.

WHY RULE THREE IS THE HARD ONE

Students arrive fluent at criticising other people's work. Almost nothing in their previous education has asked them to find the weakest point in their own conclusion and act on it. An activity that ends at the critique of someone else's claim will produce good evidence on criteria 1 and 2 and nothing at all on criterion 3 — and criterion 3 is the one an external examiner will look for first, because it is the one no other outcome in the programme covers.

In practice, criterion 3 needs someone else to attack the student's conclusion. The dialogue and reflective methods at 3.2 — the seminar, the controversy, the red team, the studio critique — are the ones that produce it; the analytical methods on their own do not.

3.3 Practice and assessed activities

Rule 2 of 3.1 asks for two linked activities on the same topic. This subsection sets out what that looks like across a semester, and what the feedback between them has to do.

WATCH OUT

Both arrangements pair an analytical method with a dialogue method. That is deliberate: the analytical method produces criteria 1 and 2, and the dialogue method is what produces criterion 3. An arrangement built from two analytical methods will leave criterion 3 with nothing to mark.

WHAT FEEDBACK BETWEEN THE RUNS MEANS

  1. After run one, students self-assess against the published rubric. Ten minutes, in writing.
  2. You return one sentence naming the single criterion to work on. Not a grade. For most students in most cohorts that sentence will name criterion 3.
  3. Run two uses a different claim and the identical rubric.
  4. Only run two is marked. Run one carries no mark, and is the reason run two is usable.

M1 Case-Based Learning

Inquiry family · worked in: Health, Medical and Life Sciences

FIELDSPECIFICATION
SituationThe two trials that disagree. Groups receive two published trials of the same intervention that reach opposite conclusions, together with their funding statements, sampling frames and outcome measures.
The contestability built inThe two trials are chosen so neither is fraudulent and neither is decisive. The disagreement sits in the outcome measure and the population sampled, so it cannot be settled by deciding which paper is better.
Run and timingTwo sessions of 90 minutes, three weeks apart. Session A appraises the evidence; independent reading between; session B produces the recommendation.
Required promptState the weakest point in the recommendation you have just made, and what you did about it.
Individual evidenceA one-page case appraisal: what the evidence actually supports, the assumption inside the case framing, the weakest point of my own recommendation, and my judgement with the confidence the evidence warrants.

WATCH OUT

Marking the recommendation rewards the group that guessed the clinically fashionable answer. Mark the appraisal.

M2 Problem-Based Learning

Inquiry family · worked in: Engineering

FIELDSPECIFICATION
SituationThe problem as stated is wrong. Teams receive a client brief asking them to reduce emissions from a process, with a data pack in which the emissions baseline was measured on a boundary that excludes the largest source.
The contestability built inThe brief is written so that solving the problem as stated produces a defensible engineering answer to the wrong question. The fault is in the framing of the baseline, not in the calculation.
Run and timingWeek 3 framing, week 6 interim reasoning log, week 9 revised definition, week 11 delivery.
Required promptWhich of your own early conclusions did the data pack not actually support, and when did you notice?
Individual evidenceA reasoning log: the problem definition I proposed, what I later found the information base could not support, and how and why my definition changed.

WATCH OUT

Teams that never revise the definition still produce a polished report. Require the interim log, dated, or the revision cannot be seen.

M3 Guided Inquiry

Inquiry family · worked in: Sciences

FIELDSPECIFICATION
SituationQuestions before method. Students are given a sustainability claim about a material or process and must write the questions they would need answered before any method is issued to them.
The contestability built inThe evidence pack released afterwards is built so that it answers some of the students' questions well, some badly, and one not at all. Which is which is the finding.
Run and timing45 minutes generating questions, 45 minutes with the evidence pack, 30 minutes writing which questions survived.
Required promptName the question your evidence cannot answer, and say what conclusion you are therefore not entitled to draw.
Individual evidenceMy question set with a rationale for each, plus a note naming the question the available evidence cannot answer and why.

WATCH OUT

Students write questions the pack obviously answers. Issue the pack only after the questions are submitted and time-stamped.

M4 Research-Method Critique

Analytical family · worked in: Education

FIELDSPECIFICATION
SituationThe half-grade. Students appraise a real study reporting that a teaching intervention raised achievement, holding the instrument, the comparison group and the attrition table.
The contestability built inThe study is competently executed and its headline is still not warranted: the achievement measure was designed alongside the intervention. Nothing is wrong with the statistics.
Run and timing60 minutes individually with the paper, 30 minutes comparing critiques, 30 minutes writing.
Required promptState one claim in the abstract that the study cannot support, and one claim of your own that your critique cannot support either.
Individual evidenceA method critique: design, measurement quality, limits of inference, and one claim in the abstract the study cannot support.

WATCH OUT

A critique that only lists limitations is a checklist. Require the student to say which limitation actually changes the conclusion.

M5 Data and Model Interrogation

Analytical family · worked in: Mathematics and Statistics

FIELDSPECIFICATION
SituationMove one assumption. Students receive the model and the data behind a published headline figure and must change one stated assumption at a time and record how far the headline moves.
The contestability built inThe model is built so one unremarkable assumption — a deflator, a threshold, an aggregation level — carries most of the headline. The arithmetic is not in dispute at any point.
Run and timing30 minutes replication, 60 minutes sensitivity runs, 30 minutes writing the note.
Required promptWhich single assumption is your own conclusion most dependent on, and how confident does that let you be?
Individual evidenceA sensitivity note: the assumption I changed, how far the conclusion moved, which assumption the headline depends on, and the confidence the result now warrants.

WATCH OUT

Students report that the result is robust because they varied only the parameters that do not matter. Require the load-bearing assumption to be named.

M6 Measurement and Field Critique

Analytical family · worked in: Agriculture, Forestry, Aquaculture, Veterinary, Environmental and Food Sciences

FIELDSPECIFICATION
SituationThe sample that stopped too shallow. Students interpret field or laboratory data — their own or supplied — on a sustainability indicator, holding the sampling protocol, the depth or interval, and the seasons covered.
The contestability built inThe protocol is competent for the purpose it was written for, and does not support the claim now being made from it. The limitation is in the sampling frame, not in the instrument.
Run and timingHalf a day of measurement or data handling, then 60 minutes writing the interpretation note.
Required promptState the strength of conclusion this measurement entitles you to, and the point at which your own inference exceeds it.
Individual evidenceAn interpretation note: what this measurement can and cannot establish, the uncertainty attached to my inference, and the strength of the conclusion I am entitled to draw.

WATCH OUT

Description is not interpretation. A note that reports the numbers accurately and draws no bounded conclusion is level 1 on criterion 4.

M7 Socratic Seminar

Dialogue family · worked in: Arts and Humanities

FIELDSPECIFICATION
SituationDefend it, then write it again. A rule-governed seminar on a contested account of a site, object or text, in which every claim must be answered under questioning and the written position is submitted afterwards.
The contestability built inThe source base is genuinely thin and partly one-sided, so a confident position cannot be defended by scholarship alone. Both a strong and a cautious reading survive the seminar.
Run and timing30 minutes preparation, 60 minutes seminar under rules, 30 minutes writing the revised position out of seminar.
Required promptWhich challenge in the seminar had most force against your position, and what did you change?
Individual evidenceA post-seminar position paper: my position before the seminar, the challenge that had most force against it, and my position after, with reasons.

WATCH OUT

Fluent speakers dominate and are rewarded for it. The mark belongs to the written revision, not to performance in the room.

M8 Structured Academic Controversy

Dialogue family · worked in: Sociology, Psychology and Human Development

FIELDSPECIFICATION
SituationArgue it, then argue against yourself. Groups of four split into two pairs on a contested empirical proposition, argue it, then switch sides before writing.
The contestability built inThe switch is compulsory, and a pair may not switch until the opposing pair accepts its restatement of their evidence as accurate. Both briefing packs are of equal quality.
Run and timing15 minutes preparation, 10 minutes each side, 10 minutes each side restating the other's evidence, 20 minutes switched, 20 minutes out of role, 15 minutes writing.
Required promptState the weakest point in the position you actually hold, and whether you have revised it, qualified it or defended it.
Individual evidenceA statement: the strongest argument on the side I did not start on, the weakest point in my own position, and my final judgement with its confidence.

WATCH OUT

The restatement step is where the competency lives, and it is the step cut when running late. Cut preparation instead. Never grade the position held.

M9 Devil's Advocate / Red Team

Dialogue family · worked in: Economics, Accounting, Business, Finance and Management

FIELDSPECIFICATION
SituationThe team paid to break it. A rotating sub-team is briefed to attack the emerging recommendation and its evidence base, and is assessed on the quality of the attack rather than on winning.
The contestability built inThe red team is given access to the same evidence and a specific instruction to find the load-bearing assumption. The recommendation cannot be defended by asserting good intentions.
Run and timing20 minutes red-team preparation, 40 minutes challenge, 30 minutes response, 20 minutes writing.
Required promptWhich objection did you find you could not answer, and what did you do about it?
Individual evidenceThe strongest objection raised against my own position, and my response to it — revision, qualification, or reasoned defence with grounds.

WATCH OUT

Red teams reward cynicism if the brief is loose. Require every objection to name the evidence or assumption it attacks.

M10 Studio Critique and Revision

Reflective family · worked in: Architecture, Design, Built Environment and Construction

FIELDSPECIFICATION
SituationThe revision is the submission. Work is pinned up and critiqued against published criteria at two intervals, and what is assessed is the revision made between them.

5.1 Two briefs on the same topic

ASSESSES KNOWLEDGE ABOUT THE TOPICASSESSES THE COMPETENCY
“Critically discuss the sustainability of low-carbon heating technologies, with examples.” A student who memorised the lecture and read nothing scores full marks. Nothing here supports four rubric levels except length and polish.“Using the manufacturer datasheet and the independent lifecycle study supplied, submit: (a) an appraisal of the test standard, measurement conditions and limits of the performance evidence, stating what it cannot establish in service; (b) one paragraph naming the operating assumption the forty per cent figure depends on, and what that assumption conceals; (c) one paragraph stating the objection to your own recommendation that you could not answer, and whether you have revised, qualified or defended it; (d) your specification recommendation with the degree of confidence the evidence warrants.” Four clauses, four criteria. It cannot be started without reading both documents.

THE CLAUSE RULE

  1. A criterion with no clause means students were never asked for that evidence, so its absence cannot be marked down.
  2. A clause with no criterion is work you asked for and will not mark.

Write the brief in four clauses, one per criterion.

CLAUSECRITERIONWHAT IT MUST REQUIRE
(a)Criterion 1An appraisal of named evidence: its source, the method that produced it, its limits, and what it cannot establish.
(b)Criterion 2One assumption inside the framing, and what that framing makes invisible or hard to recognise.
(c)Criterion 3The weakest point in the student's own conclusion, and the revision, qualification or reasoned defence that followed.
(d)Criterion 4A judgement, with the degree of confidence the evidence warrants and what would change it.

THE ORDER MATTERS

Clause (c) must come after (a) and (b) in the student's own writing, because a weakest point can only be named once a position has been taken. A brief that asks for reflection first produces sentiment; a brief that asks for it last produces evidence.

THE CLO AND THE BRIEF MUST NAME THE SAME MOVES

The CLO arrives mapped to PLO12 (1.2.1), but its wording is still yours, and a CLO written with a bare verb cannot be assessed against these four criteria. Useful stems are evaluate, interrogate, appraise, audit, critique, examine, expose and defend — but the stem alone is not enough.

Weak: Understand sustainability claims in semiconductor manufacturing.

Better: Evaluate sustainability claims concerning semiconductor manufacturing.

Strong: Evaluate the evidence supporting a sustainability claim concerning semiconductor manufacturing by examining its source, method and limitations, identifying an assumption in its framing, addressing the weakest point of the student's own conclusion, and reaching a judgement proportionate to the evidence.

Read your CLO and ask which of the four criteria a marker could point to in it. Fewer than four, and the missing criteria will have nothing to point at in the brief either.

5.2 The individual reasoning rule

WHERE THESE DESIGNS FAIL

  1. A four-clause appraisal. Two pages, one clause per criterion, on evidence the student had to actually read.
  2. A revision record. What the student changed in their own conclusion after challenge, and at whose prompting — this is where criterion 3 usually lives.
  3. A structured viva. Valid provided you mark the reasoning given under questioning, never the fluency of the delivery.

Critical Thinking is written by one student, so attribution is rarely the problem. Demand is. PLO12 is reported for each student against four criteria, and a task that does not require all four reasoning moves cannot produce them.

Two fixes that do not work. Adding the word critically to an existing task changes nothing. Raising the word limit measures stamina, and requiring more references measures volume.

Three that do. Any of these attaches individual reasoning to the task, and it is what the rubric is applied to.

5.3 Three traps when marking

TRAPLOOKS LIKEFIX
Marking the conclusionThe script that reaches the lecturer's own view scores highest; the well-reasoned opposite view scores lower.Mark the proportionality of the reasoning. Two students reaching opposite conclusions can both reach level 4, and that is what makes the mark defensible.
Marking fluencyA confident, well-structured, well-referenced report scores above a plainer one that actually interrogates the method.Mark the named evidence, the named assumption, the named weakness. Presentation is assessed under other outcomes.
Marking objections by the yardThe student who lists nine limitations scores above the one who identifies the single limitation that changes the conclusion.Mark whether the limitation identified is load-bearing, and whether the same scrutiny was turned on the student's own position.

Volume is the fourth temptation. Length, the number of references and the number of limitations named are not the measure — quality of reasoning is. See 5.5, deriving the four levels.

5.4 Placement, weight and threshold

FOUR DECISIONS

  1. Assess Critical Thinking through one component, not several. Split across three and none discriminates.
  2. Weight it 15 to 25 per cent. Below that band students under-invest and the evidence degrades.
  3. Attainment is computed from the four criteria alone. Other criteria may carry course marks. State this in the outline.
  4. Mark all four levels. The rubric is not capped for Critical Thinking. The one capping rule in the parent guidebook applies to Integrated Problem-solving assessed mid-programme, not to this competency.

The practice run and any group discussion are not marked for PLO12. They create the conditions; the individual submission carries the judgement. The week 5 run carries no mark at all.

5.5 The rubric

WHAT IS FIXED, AND WHAT YOU ADAPT

Fixed: four criteria, and their names; four levels — Limited, Developing, Proficient, Exemplary; and the judgement each criterion captures. Do not add a fifth criterion, drop one, rename a level, or substitute a different judgement.

Yours to adapt: the wording of the descriptors. Adapt every descriptor to your discipline before use. A descriptor that still says “the evidence” when your task is about a lifecycle study, a benchmark or a soil survey has not been adapted.

Attainment is added up across courses. That sum means something because the four criteria and the four levels mean the same thing in Law as in Engineering — not because the sentences are identical.

5.5.3 Deriving the four levels

  1. Write level 1 and level 4 first. Level 1 is the commonest error — the floor. Level 4 is the exemplary move — the ceiling. Writing the ends first stops the middle going flat.
  2. Then split the gap. Level 3 is level 4 minus its hardest move. Level 2 is level 1 with the first sign of the competency appearing.
  3. Differentiate by quality, not by counting. “Identifies the assumption and explains what the framing makes invisible” versus “identifies an assumption” — never “three assumptions” versus “one”.

The gradient transfers across all eight competencies: lists → connects → justifies → sees its own choice as a choice.

5.8 How to adapt the rubric

Adapting the descriptors to your discipline is required, not optional — a generic descriptor cannot be applied consistently by two markers. What must not change is the set of four criteria, their names, and the four level names. These five steps are the order to work in.

STEPWHAT TO DO
1Keep the four criteria and their names unchanged: Evidence; Framing and assumptions; Own position; Warranted judgement. Do not add a fifth, drop one, or reorder them.
2Rewrite each descriptor in disciplinary language. Name the actual study, the actual datasheet, the actual assumption in your task — not “the evidence”. The judgement the criterion captures stays the same; only the words change.
3Write level 1 and level 4 first, then split the quality gap between them to create levels 2 and 3. The two extremes are far easier to describe truthfully, and they fix the scale the middle has to fit inside.
4Differentiate levels by quality of reasoning, not by counting references, limitations or examples. One limitation shown to change the conclusion is level 4; three listed without that is level 3.
5Test each criterion against the one rule. Could a student score full marks on it without using the competency? If yes, it is measuring writing or effort — rewrite it. If the brief has no clause asking for it, amend the brief.

KEEP THE FOUR INDEPENDENT

If two criteria always rise and fall together, they are one criterion written twice. Name each criterion for the judgement it captures, never for a feature of the output: “Own position”, not “the reflection paragraph”.

6.4 Moderation, second marking and calibration

MODERATION CHECKLIST

THE CALIBRATION QUESTIONS

  1. What evidence in the student's work justified the level selected?
  2. Which descriptor produced the disagreement?
  3. Was the disagreement about disciplinary content, or about the reasoning?
  4. Does the descriptor need rewording before marking begins — and is it the descriptor, not the criterion?

Ask these four of every calibration script, in this order. They are also the questions to ask when two markers disagree.

WATCH OUT

The commonest calibration failure in this competency is a marker scoring criterion 4 low because the student's conclusion differs from their own. Include a script of that kind deliberately, and settle it before marking rather than at the appeal.

C1 Facilitation principles

FIVE PRINCIPLES

  1. Keep participants working on their own course. Generic examples are for the first twenty minutes only; after that, every activity is done on the course the participant brought.
  2. Do not let anyone begin with the rubric. Assessment evidence must be defined before descriptors are adapted, and a participant who starts at the rubric will produce four adjectives.
  3. When a participant proposes a broad SDG activity, ask what exactly each student must do that demonstrates the competency. The primary SDG is already fixed (1.2.1); it is not theirs to change.
  4. When a participant adds the word “critically” to an existing task, ask them to name the four moves explicitly, in the task, in writing.
  5. Use different discipline examples around the room. The construct stays constant even when the evidence changes, and hearing that is what convinces a sceptical participant.

WATCH OUT

The single most common outcome of a badly run day is a room full of well-designed criterion 1 and 2 tasks with no criterion 3 anywhere. Watch for it from Module 3 onward and intervene early: ask every participant, out loud, where in their design a student is required to find the weakest point in their own conclusion.

D3 Where the criteria come from

STATEMENT OF PROVENANCE

Criteria 1 to 3 operationalise the three observable behaviours the UPM ESD Guidebook gives for this competency, which themselves render the abilities in the UNESCO (2017) definition: criteria 1 and 2 the questioning of norms, practices and opinions; criterion 3 reflection on one's own values, perceptions and actions. Criterion 4 is added so that the analysis resolves into a decision; it also carries the third ability, taking a position in the sustainability discourse.

The division of the first ability into two criteria is a UPM decision, made because evidence quality and framing are separately markable and students commonly reach one without the other. UNESCO does not prescribe how many criteria a rubric has, what the levels are called, how attainment is thresholded, or how competency data is aggregated. Four criteria, four named levels, the year-of-study threshold and the aggregation rule are UPM design decisions and are defensible as such. Do not present them as UNESCO requirements.

END PRODUCT

The intended result is not a lecturer who can define Critical Thinking. It is a lecturer who can redesign one real course so that students practise, demonstrate and receive feedback on Critical Thinking through authentic disciplinary work — and can show an examiner the script that proves it.
